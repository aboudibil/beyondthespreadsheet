Some public problems resist every reasonable fix. Usually the money isn't the issue. The parts are connected. A proof of concept makes those connections visible, so you can test them.

Does this sound familiar?

Problems a spreadsheet can't see.

  • ◆ Every push fails, until one suddenly works, and no one can say why.
  • ◆ Two reforms each did nothing alone. Together they did a lot.
  • ◆ The same programme, launched in a different order, gave a different result.
  • ◆ The average improved, but the people it was meant for fell further behind.
  • ◆ The effect was large on paper and a fraction of that on the ground.

Patterns like these show up across domains: health, skills, culture, infrastructure, public services. They come from structure, so a model of the structure can show where they come from.

What a proof of concept is

A working model, not a report.

Openable
It runs in a browser, with nothing to install. You change the policy levers and see what follows, and why.
Built on named theory
Every mechanism traces to published work you can check, cite and dispute.
Native to the problem
Its mechanisms are built for your problem, not relabelled from another domain.
Honest about its limits
Assumptions are declared and robustness is tested. It helps you reason about a problem. It does not forecast.
How one is built

Four steps, two human checks.

1 · Frame the problem
State the public problem and the one question the model must help answer.
2 · Map the actors and the theory
Who is involved, who decides, and which published theories explain how the problem behaves.
3 · Build it native
Build the stocks, feedback loops and links for this problem. An AI assistant can draft and search.
4 · Check, then check again
First check: every reference is real and says what it is claimed to say. Second check: conclusions survive structural tests and a parameter sweep.

The full method, with its ready-to-paste prompts, is open to anyone. You don't need to know anything else on this site to use it.
